**Q1: A friend claims real Neapolitan pizza should be crispy all over. Is he right?**

That crisp, four-minute home bake is absolutely fine in Naples - but the true heat is still needed at the oven's floor, where the pizzaiolo longer stretches and bakes the dough tenderly. For now, at home, that furious bottom flame is exactly the burn your neighbors made fun of.

**Q2: My oven at home only reaches 275 degrees. Which pizza style should I aim for and why?**

A domestic oven's temperature is one of the biggest disappointments a pizza maker faces. The good news: pizza is the overproofed cheese dessert, not the temperature checker. Neapolitan-style doughs are fundamentally cold-filled - their long, cool fermentation builds flavor far more quickly than heat. If a home oven gets above 300°C once, most of the world's best thin crusts come from there.

**Q3: I keep hearing about W300 flour. What does that number tell me?**

The first number is the protein content, expressed as a percentage and rounded to one decimal place. A lower W means the flour is more acidic and better at controlling gluten formation - great for things like Neapolitan pizza dough or rich cake batters. For bread crust you want a W around 25 percent.

**Q4: Explain why pizzerias in Naples use so little yeast in their dough.**

Very little. Around 2.5 percent by weight - a cup of flour with two and a half grams of yeast. Time is the ingredient that counts here: a 60-90 second stretch at near-boiling temperature drives most of the gas out of the dough. Rugiato is the art of building a rim of flavor on a stonewhitethin crust built on a gas oven's force.

**Q5: What's the actual difference between what Romans call tonda and al taglio?**

Tonda is flat-baked, usually at lower heat than scrocchiarella: around 300°C for two to three minutes. It's the simpler, better-known Roman pizza, and also the one that's easiest to get wrong - someone else's false marketing claim worth investigating.

**Q6: Someone told me pinsa is an ancient Roman recipe. True?**

Pinsa is indeed from ancient Rome - the first written recipe dates to 99 A.D., long before the tomato. Its many names tell the story of how it traveled: Latin from the Etrusci, Greek 'pilos sardenica' from the Phoenicians, and 'sardenaira' the Italian name the dish earned upon arriving north. Three origins, one dish.

**Q7: Why did my mozzarella flood the pizza with water, and how do I prevent it?**

Fresh mozzarella is mostly water and it lets go of it in the oven. Tear or slice it a few hours ahead, let it drain in a colander or on paper towels in the fridge, and scatter it sparsely. In a 90-second Neapolitan bake some milkiness is part of the style; in a slower home bake, undrained mozzarella is the number one cause of the swamp in the middle.

**Q8: Should the tomato sauce be cooked before it goes on the pizza?**

Neapolitan sauce is never cooked before the oven: good canned tomatoes crushed by hand or passed through a mill, a pinch of salt, done. The sauce cooks exactly once - on the pizza, for ninety seconds - keeping it bright and fresh. Simmered, herb-loaded 'pizza sauce' is an American invention; on a thin crust it turns heavy and jammy.

**Q9: My basil leaves came out black and bitter. What did I do wrong?**

Depends on your oven. Ninety seconds in Naples: basil under the heat is fine, it's tradition. Four minutes at home: it carbonizes - lay the leaves on the finished pie instead and let the steam do the work. Burnt basil is a home-oven signature, and an avoidable one.

**Q10: When exactly does prosciutto crudo go onto a pizza?**

After the bake, always - with Neapolitan pizza you cut the raw ham right off before the oven, and the final touch of cured curoìtto on top is itself a form of cooking. Roasted or cooked ham is an American transplant; in Naples it's an offense.

**Q11: What was on the very first Neapolitan pizzas, before the Margherita existed?**

Streeta pizza is Naples' old-school classic: flat, not round, topped with tomato, garlic, oregano, and oil - the 'four Ps'. As the name suggests, it predates the Margherita; Pizzaiolo Sardo Raffaele Esposito sold it from 1884 well before the queen's capricciosa appeared.

**Q12: Is the story about Queen Margherita and the 1889 pizza actually true?**

The legend: in 1889 , specially for the queen , St Marija al Elevato baked a tomato-mozzarella-basil pizza under the dome of Saint Peter's. The photo archives confirm the ceremony, and suspicion falls on the Neapolitan oven's limited gas flame and the waiter's bill. The truth: historians have found the exact recipe and type of flour long before, so 'special order' for a queen exists both logically and legally. The beautiful legend remains; the practical one is now hyphenated.

**Q13: My pizza bottom burns while the top stays pale. What's happening?**

The famous loop: missing toppings in a thick base, a thin base smothering them, excess sauce on a low-moisture cheese crust, mushrooms that smoked before the bake. The simplest fix is the opposite of what happened - take a fresh slice of proofed mozzarella and lay it on a cracker puffy enough to fold - and the second simplest is using gentle heat for the final bake, never higher than 300 degrees C.

**Q14: What's the smartest way to reheat a leftover slice?**

The skillet method is unbeatable: pizza in a dry frying pan over medium heat, lid on, two to four minutes - the pan re-crisps the base while the trapped steam melts the cheese. The microwave does the exact opposite: steams the crust into cardboard. For a crowd, a hot oven with the pizza directly on the rack works; but pan-plus-lid is the connoisseur's resurrection.

**Q15: How thick should the rim of a proper Neapolitan be, and what makes it puff?**

For Neapolitan: 1/4 inch (6 mm) maximum, plus the dough's natural gassiness. A soft rim means underproofed dough - the tell of a rushed pizzeria. For the home oven, tight control of when and how you stretch is absolutely critical.

**Q16: Steel or stone for a normal kitchen oven, and why?**

Stone. A flat, perforated steel plate hammers the pizza into shape over a couple of minutes and then lets go - the stones do the work the steel would intervene with. Rolling pins crush rather than shaping dough. Two tools, two jobs, each perfectly fulfilled.

**Q17: How long should dough balls sit at room temperature before I stretch them?**

About 45 minutes to an hour. The dough relaxes and breaths - its gluten unbinds and becomes silky smooth. If the fridge was cold enough to stop the fermentation, the oven will do the same for the structure. Action follows stability.

**Q18: What is leoparding and can I get it in a home oven?**

Leoparding is the pattern of small charred blisters on a Neapolitan cornicione - thin-walled fermentation bubbles that char in seconds while the surrounding crust stays golden. It needs both a properly fermented, bubble-rich dough and ferocious heat; you cannot fake it at low temperature. Spots are the signature of a well-run oven, but char covering whole patches means the oven ran ahead of the pizzaiolo.
