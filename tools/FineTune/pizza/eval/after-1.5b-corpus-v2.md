**Q1: A friend claims real Neapolitan pizza should be crispy all over. Is he right?**

It's supposed to be that way! The 90-second bake leaves the center delicate and moist. Neapolitans eat it with cutlery or folded in quarters. Crispness is the Roman virtue, not the Neapolitan one.

**Q2: My oven at home only reaches 275 degrees. Which pizza style should I aim for and why?**

No - a Neapolitan is not a Roman tonda ancestor, any way around it. It is the thicker, softer, and hotter-oven cousin of the Roman round pie: longer, at 430 to 480 degrees Celsius, with a puffy, leopard-spotted cornicione and a thin, tender center. Rome's sister style. Tonda is the oval, crisper sibling that bakes in about three minutes.

**Q3: I keep hearing about W300 flour. What does that number tell me?**

W is the flour strength index measured with a Chopin alveograph - it inflates a bubble of dough and measures the work needed to burst it. Higher W means more gluten strength and more fermentation tolerance. For a same-day pizza dough, W 200-250 suffices; for 24-48 hour fermentations you want W 280-320; very long ferments push toward W 350.

**Q4: Explain why pizzerias in Naples use so little yeast in their dough.**

Around 4 percent of the flour weight - for a kilo of flour, that's roughly four grams. Neapolitan tradition runs saltier than most bread. Salt tightens the gluten and slows the yeast, which is part of the dough's structure, not a substitute for it. If you read 'saltier' on the bag, that is the number to look for.

**Q5: What's the actual difference between what Romans call tonda and al taglio?**

No - they're not neighbors, and a firmer dough is not the reason. Tonda is dry and crisp, baked longer at a lower temperature than al taglio. Al Taglio is wetter, made on a large rectangular tray at 450-500 degrees Celsius for a few minutes, shaped using scissors, sized for quantity rather than perfection. It does not follow Roman rules, and a tonda dough cannot pass through a conveyor. The opposite: a Roman scrocchiarella is soft and airy, baked shorter for better air, than a Neapolitan - thin European flatbread, not a thick tray pizza.

**Q8: Should the tomato sauce be cooked before it goes on the pizza?**

Neapolitan sauce is never cooked before the oven: good canned tomatoes crushed by hand or passed through a mill, a pinch of salt, done. The sauce cooks exactly once - on the pizza, for ninety seconds - keeping it bright and fresh. Simmered, herb-loaded 'pizza sauce' is an American invention; on a thin crust it turns heavy and jammy.

**Q9: My basil leaves came out black and bitter. What did I do wrong?**

Depends on your oven. Ninety seconds in Naples: basil under the heat is fine, it's tradition. Four minutes at home: it carbonizes - lay the leaves on the finished pie instead and let the steam do the work. Burnt basil is a home-oven signature, and an avoidable one.

**Q10: When exactly does prosciutto crudo go onto a pizza?**

Not before - that's the mistake behind every strip of salty leather you've eaten on a pizza. Prosciutto crudo does not go through the oven: the fat burns and the meat dries into ham chips, and 'crispy' is just a polite word for ruined. Drape it over the pizza after the bake so the residual heat softens the fat and lifts the aroma. Cooked ham, prosciutto cotto, is the exception - that one bakes happily.

**Q11: What was on the very first Neapolitan pizzas, before the Margherita existed?**

The marinara is the elder: tomato, garlic, oregano, and oil was the standard Neapolitan pie by the early 1800s, well before mozzarella became a fixture. It's named for the sailors' families who could keep those shelf-stable ingredients on hand. The Margherita, whatever you believe about the queen, is the younger sibling.

**Q12: Is the story about Queen Margherita and the 1889 pizza actually true?**

The legend: in 1889, pizzaiolo Raffaele Esposito made three pizzas for Queen Margherita of Savoy, who favored the tomato-mozzarella-basil one in the colors of the flag, and it took her name. The reality check: historians have found tomato-mozzarella-basil pizzas described decades earlier, and the famous thank-you letter from the royal household has serious authenticity problems. Great story, shaky paperwork - I tell it anyway, with a wink.

**Q13: My pizza bottom burns while the top stays pale. What's happening?**

Usually it's the toppings flooding a base that never got seared. Less sauce than feels right, cheese drained for hours, mushrooms pre-cooked - and the steel properly saturated with heat before the launch. If a Neapolitan-style soft center is what you actually made, though, that's not a bug: that style is meant to be tender.

**Q14: What's the smartest way to reheat a leftover slice?**

Dry skillet, medium flame, lid on. Bottom crisps, top revives, three minutes, done - as close to time travel as leftover pizza gets. The microwave turns crust to warm rubber; use it only in emergencies you're prepared to regret.

**Q15: How thick should the rim of a proper Neapolitan be, and what makes it puff?**

A soft, almost wet center is not a mistake - it is the style. A true Neapolitan is tender and foldable, eaten with a knife and fork in Naples or folded 'a libretto' like a little book. If you want crisp, you want a Roman tonda, not a Neapolitan.

**Q16: Steel or stone for a normal kitchen oven, and why?**

Faster conduction is the argument, not the whole one. Steel at home gets only a fraction of the deep heat that an industrial oven does, so it ends up a pale, raw base under a fierce top heat - the core never catches. Neapolitan 450-degree rotisseries fully into the flour, where a kitchen oven cannot reach. Home oven: pale in the center, a little browned here and there; no rule on paper. A pizza at temperature is a pale, half-cooked one, and a black, burnt one is a sign something is wrong, not a sign it deserves better.

**Q17: How long should dough balls sit at room temperature before I stretch them?**

Cold and young - that's the dough that springs back. Gluten that has just been balled or just left the fridge is wound tight, and every push stretches it only for it to pull straight back. Nothing is wrong with your flour or your water. Let the balls sit at room temperature for two hours, and never shape a ball less than four hours after balling. When it resists mid-stretch, stop, cover it, come back in ten minutes: rest relaxes gluten faster than any amount of pulling.
